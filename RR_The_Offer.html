01. The Real Number

We are paying $124 to collect about $120.

Before any argument about offers or positioning, there is an arithmetic problem, and everything else in this document exists because of it. Here it is in five steps.

1

What does a member pay us?

$55 per month, cancel any time. That is the only offer a new person sees today.

2

How long do they stay?

Two to three months is what we see in practice. That puts the total we ever collect from one member at somewhere between $110 and $165. Not per month. In total, for their whole life with us.

3

What does it cost to get one?

The best result in the history of the ad account is $124.39 per signup, from the Newark event campaign. Every other campaign we have run has come in at $541.61. So $124 is not the normal price. It is the record.

4

So where does that leave us?

On our best day ever, we spend $124 to collect $110 to $165. That is break even if the member stays three months and a loss if they stay two. On a normal day we spend $541 to collect the same $120. There is no version of this that scales.

5

Is that an advertising problem?

No. The current campaign is producing 3.40% click through at $0.85 per click, the strongest numbers this account has ever recorded, into a warm audience that already knows Brian. The traffic is cheap, it is qualified, and it arrives. What it arrives to is worth $120.

The ads are not the constraint. The offer is. We cannot keep paying $124 for someone who will hand us $120 and leave.
A note on the retention chart, because it seems to say something different. Skool's cohort table measures the percentage of value a group still produces, not the percentage of people still there, and it is dominated by the October 2025 launch cohort, which entered at $27.6K of monthly value against $2.3K to $9.6K for every cohort since. Those are people who followed the channel for years before there was anything to buy. They behave nothing like someone who arrived last week from an ad. Both readings are true at the same time. The value curve holds up because of the launch group, and the member we buy with paid traffic still leaves in month two or three. When the question is how much we can afford to spend acquiring someone, the second number is the one that applies.

What one member is worth against what one member costs

Monthly figure is $55 multiplied by an observed two to three month stay. Annual figure is twelve months at $55 less 40%. Cost line is the best cost per signup on record, from the Newark campaign.

02. The Discount Illusion

A 40% discount on money we have never once collected.

This is the part that tends to get read backwards, so it is worth being very literal about it.

Twelve months at $55 is $660. Take 40% off and the price becomes $396. Written down like that it looks as though we just gave away $264, and that is the instinctive objection to annual pricing everywhere.

We never had $264. We had $110 to $165, and only if the member stayed the full two or three months. The 40% is being subtracted from a number that has never appeared in our accounts, from a single member, at any point. It is a discount on a hypothetical.

03. Why a Year

The year is not how they pay. The year is what they are buying.

The money argument above is the easy half. This is the half that decides whether any of it actually works.

What we sell is change. Not information, not videos, not access to a feed. Somebody arrives because they want their life to work differently, and everything Brian teaches is built around that.

Change does not happen in thirty days. Nobody rebuilds how they think, how they react, or what they believe is available to them inside one billing cycle. It takes months, and it takes months of contact with someone who has already done it and with other people moving in the same direction at the same time. That is the actual product, and time is not a side effect of it. Time is the mechanism.

So we have been attaching a monthly cancel button to a promise that needs a year to keep. Those two things are in direct conflict with each other. We ask people to commit to nothing, and then we are surprised when they are gone before anything has changed.

A monthly member is asked twelve times a year whether they still feel like continuing. Every one of those moments is a chance to quit, and people quit in the weeks where nothing dramatic is happening, which in real change is most weeks.

Someone who pays for the year has made one decision instead of twelve. And they did not make that decision to us. They made it to themselves. The payment is not the commitment, it is the evidence of it. That is why this cannot be solved with a longer free trial or a better onboarding email. Commitments that cost nothing are not commitments, and everyone involved knows it, including the member.

There is a second half to it that is just as important. The year is not twelve months of access. It is twelve months of Brian and the community working on the same person, in the same direction, for long enough that the work lands. Sold monthly, the member is gone before the community has had the chance to do the one thing a community exists to do, which is to be there on the day someone would otherwise have given up. The monthly plan is quietly undermining the entire reason the community was built.

Which means the discount is not a bribe, it is the price of the commitment. We are not cutting the price to make it easier to say yes. We are cutting it because we are asking for something harder than a monthly subscription asks for, and that is worth paying for. The member trades flexibility for a real shot at the result. We trade margin for their commitment. Both sides of that are better off, and it is the only version where the promise and the payment terms are pointing the same way.
04. Keep or Kill Monthly

The hard question, argued both ways.

This is the part of the decision with real risk in it, so it should not be waved through. Here is the honest case on each side and then what I would actually do.

The case for removing monthly

  • Two prices on a page is not an offer, it is a survey. Faced with $55 now or $396 now, almost everyone picks the small number, and they pick it without thinking about which one is better for them.
  • A programme with a start date and a group cannot have people arriving on random Tuesdays. The cohort is a large part of what makes it work.
  • It makes every ad, page and email simpler. One offer, one decision, one thing to say.
  • Monthly is the mechanism producing the churn we are trying to fix. Keeping it visible means keeping the problem.

The case for keeping it

  • Every current member is on monthly. Taking it away from them breaks what they bought and would cause exactly the churn spike we are trying to avoid.
  • Some people who genuinely want in cannot put $396 on a card. Losing them completely is worse than having them at $55.
  • If we ever want a low commitment entry point again, rebuilding one from nothing is harder than keeping the one that exists.
  • It is reversible. Hiding an option can be undone in an afternoon. Cancelling everyone's plan cannot.
Recommendation: monthly stays. It just stops being a choice at the point of sale.
1
Existing members are not touched. At all. Ever. Everyone currently on monthly keeps monthly, at their current price, permanently. Nothing in this proposal applies to them, and the first line of any internal communication about it should say so.
2
New traffic sees one offer. Paid traffic and launch traffic land on a page where the twelve month programme is the only thing available. Not annual highlighted with monthly sitting underneath it. Annual only. The moment both are visible, the decision stops being about the programme and becomes about the smaller number.
3
Monthly becomes an objection handler, not an entry point. If someone says the year is too much, monthly is what we offer in the reply email or on the call. It still exists and it still catches people we would otherwise lose. It is just not on the shelf.

And the affordability problem gets solved without reopening the door. $396 in one payment, or three payments of $147. Both are a twelve month commitment. The only thing that changes is the cash flow, and the commitment stays intact, because a payment plan cannot be cancelled and a subscription can. That is the whole distinction, and it is the reason a payment plan is a legitimate answer to "I cannot afford it" while a monthly plan is not.

My honest view, for what it is worth. If it were purely my call I would remove monthly from the sale entirely and never show it to new traffic again. Not because $55 is too cheap, but because it is the option that lets somebody avoid making a decision, and avoiding the decision is exactly what they are already doing when they cancel in month two. The three rules above reach the same place with a safety valve on it, so nothing breaks for the people already inside and nothing is irreversible if we are wrong.
05. How It Gets Sold

The event cadence is the enrollment window.

Two things came up in the last conversation that fit this proposal exactly, and both of them change how the ad budget should be spent.

The first is that the big jumps in sales have come from building the email list and then selling through it, not from ads closing sales directly. That is correct, and it is not an argument against paid traffic. It is an argument about what paid traffic is for. An ad that tries to close a $396 decision on a cold click will not do it. An ad that puts someone on a list, where they spend two weeks getting to know Brian before anyone asks them for anything, will. The list and the offer are not competing strategies. The list is the front half of the same funnel, and what it sells at the end of it has to be worth the effort of building it. A list nurtured for three weeks and then pointed at a $55 monthly subscription is a lot of work for very little.

The second is that the event launches are planned every two to three months. That is the enrollment window, and it explains something in the ad account that we have never been able to account for any other way. The cheapest signups we have ever bought came from the Newark event campaign at $124.39. Similar audience, same landing page, no event behind it, and the cost was $541.61. The variable was not the targeting and it was not the creative. It was that something was happening and there was a reason to act that week.

Ads should not run flat all year

They should run hot in the weeks before an enrollment window and go quiet between them. That is cheaper than always on, it performs better, and it matches the only structure in our data that has ever produced conversions at a sane price. Spending the same budget evenly across twelve months is the version we have been running, and it is the version that produced $541 signups.

The list is the asset, the window is the event, the programme is the sale

Each one needs the other two. A list with nothing worth launching becomes a newsletter. A launch with no list has nobody to sell to. A programme with no launch date is just a product page. Run as one system they compound, and every window makes the next one cheaper because the list carries over.

The first window should be treated as the test, not the rollout

One window, one offer, one cohort, everything measured. How many leads, what they cost, how many converted, at what price. That gives us real numbers for the annual instead of the estimates in this document, and it is a small enough bet that being wrong costs a few weeks rather than a quarter.

06. What We Owe Them

If we ask for a year, it has to be shaped like a year.

This is what makes $396 honest rather than clever, and it is worth saying plainly that none of it is invented to justify a price.

A twelve month programme that turns out to be the same feed with a longer invoice is a refund request waiting to happen. Five things have to exist, and each one has a specific job.

1
A start date Everyone in an enrollment window begins on the same day. This is what turns a purchase into a beginning, and it is what makes the group a group rather than a room of strangers at different stages.
2
A path, not a library Phases in order, so that month four is a different experience from month one. Right now nobody on our side is responsible for making later months feel different, so for a lot of members they do not. That is a large part of why they leave.
3
Check-ins at day 7, day 30 and day 90 The first month is where the largest single drop happens in every cohort we have. Nothing else in the programme matters as much as somebody noticing when a new member goes quiet in week two, and reaching out while it still counts.
4
The group itself The people who started on the same day are the reason anyone is still there in month seven, when the initial motivation has worn off and nothing dramatic is happening. This is the part that cannot be replaced with content.
5
Brian at fixed moments On a schedule people can plan their month around, rather than whenever it happens. Predictable access is worth more than frequent access, because it is the thing a member can point to when they are deciding whether this is real.
This is the same work that fixes churn on the monthly plan. Every item above would improve retention whether we ever sell a single annual subscription or not. The difference is that right now there is no money to pay for building it, because a member is worth $120. The annual offer is what funds the thing that makes the annual offer deliverable, and that is the order it has to happen in.
07. Risks & Open Questions

What could go wrong, and what I do not know yet.

Nothing here is a reason not to do it. They are things that should be decided before the first window rather than during it.

Cash up front is not the same as MRR, and the dashboard will look worse

An annual payment does not display as monthly recurring revenue. Skool's MRR chart will go down in the months after we switch, even while the business is meaningfully better, because the money arrived as one payment instead of twelve. We should agree on this in advance so that nobody reads that chart in November and concludes the plan failed. The number to watch instead is cash collected per enrollment window against ad spend in that window.

Refunds and chargebacks

A year taken in advance and not delivered on becomes a refund request, and in the worst case a public one. This is the real risk in the proposal and it is why section 06 is not decoration. We should also set the refund window deliberately rather than discovering our policy during the first complaint.

Revenue becomes lumpy

Enrollment windows mean months with a lot of cash and months with very little. That is manageable, but only if it is planned for. It is a different rhythm from a subscription business and it should not come as a surprise to whoever is looking at the bank account in week six.

The tenure figure is the foundation and we have never measured it properly

Everything in section 01 rests on members staying two to three months. That is what we observe, but we have never measured it cleanly by traffic source, and someone who arrives from an ad almost certainly behaves differently from someone who arrives from a YouTube video. If that number is wrong, the size of the opportunity changes even though the direction does not.

1
Can Skool hide the monthly option for traffic arriving on a specific link? If it can, this is straightforward. If it cannot, the sale happens off Skool instead: ad to landing page or VSL to checkout, and the Skool invitation goes out after payment. That is arguably the better structure anyway, but it is more to build, so the answer changes the timeline. Needs Dominic
2
What is the refund policy on the annual, and what refund rate would we accept? Decided before launch, written on the page, and agreed internally so that the first request does not turn into an argument. Owner to define
3
Do existing monthly members get offered the programme, and at what price? They are the warmest audience we have and they have already paid us. There is a version of this where the first enrollment window is offered to them first, which would also tell us whether the offer works before we spend anything on traffic. Owner to define
4
What is the date of the next event? Everything else here is built backwards from it. Ad budget, lead capture, nurture sequence and enrollment window all key off that one date, and none of it can be scheduled until it exists. Needs the events calendar
5
Can we measure median tenure by traffic source? The single number this whole document depends on. Worth getting properly even if it takes a manual export, because it sets what we can afford to pay for a member and therefore how much we can spend. Owner to define

Why this is what scaling actually looks like

Nobody scales paid traffic into a fifty five dollar cancel anytime subscription. The money goes out faster than it comes back, every single time, and no amount of better creative fixes arithmetic. That is not a judgement about our ads or our product. It is the same constraint for everyone buying traffic against a low ticket monthly price.

The businesses that buy traffic profitably in this market all solve it the same way. They collect enough on day one to pay for the click, and they sell a commitment long enough for the product to actually work. Those two things are not separate tactics. They are the same decision, because the only reason a customer will pay up front is that they are buying something that takes time, and the only reason a business can afford to buy customers is that it collects before it delivers.

We already have the hard parts. A real audience, a person they trust, cheap qualified clicks, an 86% active community and an event calendar. What we are missing is an offer that can carry the cost of growth. That is the gap this closes, and it is the last thing standing between the traffic we already know how to buy and a business that can afford to buy more of it.
